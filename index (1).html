Which pages are slipping? Ranking content for review with pre-decision search signals

Sanniya Aslam · FlyRank ML Internship capstone · Lane 2, refresh and content opportunity scoring · October 2026

Abstract

Content teams can review only a few pages each week, so the order of the review queue matters. This study asks whether search signals that are known before a reviewer looks at a page can rank which pages lose clicks over the next two weeks better than a hand-written rule. Using one month (March 2026) of pseudonymized Search Console data from the FlyRank internship warehouse, features were built from days 1–15 and the label from days 16–31, and a stale-and-low-CTR rule was compared with logistic regression and a random forest under a client-grouped holdout. The rule reached a Precision@50 of 0.20, while the random forest reached 0.88 in the main run (0.74 in a later re-run) with a ROC AUC of about 0.91, and the logistic regression reached 0.78. The evidence is observational, covers one month, and the label is partly mechanical, so the result supports human prioritisation of review and nothing more: it is not a causal claim about refreshing pages and not a model of Google’s ranking.

1. Introduction and problem statement

A content reviewer managing a large portfolio has to choose which pages to look at first. A false alarm costs reviewer hours on a page that was fine; a missed page loses visibility that nobody notices. The decision this work supports is therefore: given limited review capacity, which pages should be opened first?

The research question is narrow on purpose. Can observable, pre-decision signals (impressions, click-through rate, average position, days since the last update and word count) put pages that go on to lose clicks nearer the top of a queue than a simple, readable rule does? The comparison to a rule is the point: a model is only worth using if it beats something a person could write in one line.

2. Data

Release. The FlyRank internship warehouse (pseudonymized release v20260703, a star schema with salted hash keys). All client, content, keyword and URL identifiers are hashes; nothing in this paper identifies a client, a page or a query.

Tables. The daily performance fact table (partition month=2026-03), the content dimension and the client dimension.

Window. March 2026, split in time. Days 1–15 supply every feature. Days 16–31 are used only to build the label. The final warehouse month was treated as a sealed test month and was never used.

Kept and excluded. Rows were kept only where Search Console data was available (checked with IS TRUE) and where a page had impressions in the first half. The product’s own decision fields (last optimized date, optimization eligible date) were excluded because they record a decision someone had already made, which would leak the outcome. After joining the two halves the slice has 141,467 page rows; the Week 5 split used 129,917 for training and 11,550 for testing.

3. Methodology

Label

A page is labelled declining if its clicks in days 16–31 are lower than its clicks in days 1–15. About 20% of pages carry this label. It is a short-window proxy for decline, not a measure of long-term content health.

Features

Five first-half-only features: impressions, click-through rate, average position, days since last update (negative values clipped to zero) and word count. Every feature name carries a _fh suffix as a guard against borrowing from the second half.

Baseline

The rule flags a page when it has gone at least 180 days without an update, has at least 100 impressions and a click-through rate below 2%, and ranks flagged pages by impressions times staleness. Before encoding it, two of its signals were checked on the data: the position-to-CTR relationship held (CTR fell steadily from the top positions to position 50 and beyond), while staleness gave a mixed picture and was kept only as a coarse gate.

Models and validation

A logistic regression (readable coefficients) and a random forest (200 trees, depth 6, balanced classes) were trained and scored on the same held-out pages as the rule, with Precision@50 as the main metric because a reviewer reads a short list, not the whole portfolio. The split is grouped by client with 25% of clients held out, so no client appears on both sides.

Leakage checks

No feature correlates strongly with the label (largest absolute value 0.21, table below), and no feature is computed from the second-half clicks. A before-and-after test showed why grouping matters:

Both learned models ranked clearly above the rule on this slice, and the random forest’s ROC AUC was stable across runs at about 0.91. Precision@50 was not stable: it moved from 0.88 to 0.74 between runs. The most likely reason is that the warehouse query returned rows in no fixed order, so the same random seed drew different held-out clients each time. The honest summary is “well above the rule, with a top-50 precision somewhere between 0.74 and 0.88”, not a single figure.

The random forest is only modestly better than the logistic regression, so complexity is buying a little here, not a lot, and the logistic regression remains the easier of the two to explain to a reviewer.

Where the model is wrong

Missed pages (false negatives) were visible pages, with roughly 1,300 to 12,500 half-month impressions, near-zero click-through rate and good average positions, that the model scored only around 0.70. False alarms were the opposite: pages with 5 to 16 impressions that scored above 0.90. A click-through rate on a handful of impressions is noise, and the model treats it as if it were as reliable as one measured over thousands.

5. Limitations and honest framing

  • Observational, one month. One March 2026 slice. Nothing here shows the result holds in other months, seasons or for new clients, and the warehouse is an unbalanced panel in which clients differ in history length.
  • The label is partly mechanical. Decline is defined by comparing clicks in two halves, and the features include first-half clicks (through click-through rate). A page with no first-half clicks cannot be labelled declining, and pages with unusually many first-half clicks are more likely to fall back. Some of the model’s skill is therefore likely to be regression toward the mean rather than a genuine decline signal.
  • Small counts are fragile. The highest-scored pages in the final queue had about 15 to 19 impressions in the half-month, where a single click changes the label. These are the least reliable rows in the output.
  • Data quirk. Some content update dates fall after the reference date, giving negative staleness. They were clipped to zero, not repaired, and this is part of why staleness behaved as a weak signal.
  • No causal claim. The work does not show that refreshing a page makes it recover, and it says nothing about how Google ranks pages. A high score means “worth a human look first”.

6. Ranked recommendations

The model was turned into a queue with a reason code and an action label for each page. Across the 11,550 held-out pages in the Week 7 run it produced 4,830 pages marked investigate, 19 marked monitor and 6,701 with no action. No page reached the stale-specific refresh now tier, and the top of the queue was made of recently updated, low-volume pages, so staleness was not what drove the highest scores.

  1. Put a volume floor in front of the queue. Because the top ranks are tiny-volume pages, require a minimum number of impressions (for example 100 in the window) before a page can be flagged. This is a suggestion from the error analysis and has not yet been tested.
  2. Review the “investigate” tier by volume as well as score. Start with pages where the traffic at stake is large enough to matter.
  3. Check for outside explanations first. Seasonality, a competitor’s new page, a change in search-result layout and tracking problems can each look like decline.
  4. Treat any refresh as a test. Compare refreshed pages with comparable untouched pages at 30 and 60 days, instead of assuming the refresh worked.
  5. Revalidate on a schedule. Retrain or recheck if the declining rate drifts well away from about 20%, if Precision@50 on a fresh month falls below the range seen here, if a client with a very different traffic profile is added, or if three months pass without a check.

What should not be automated

  • Publishing, unpublishing or deleting pages based on the score.
  • Editing page content without a person reviewing that specific page.
  • Presenting the score as a prediction of Google’s ranking behaviour.
  • Promising a client that a refresh will recover traffic.

The cost thinking is simple. A false alarm costs reviewer time; a miss costs visibility nobody noticed. With a fixed weekly review budget, precision at the top of the list is what matters, which is why the reported metric is Precision@50.

7. Reproducibility

All work lives in the public repository github.com/SanniyaAslam/flyrank-ml-internship, under work/notebooks/:

  • w03_data_contract: grain, row counts and availability checks.
  • w04_baseline_score: signal checks and the rule baseline.
  • w05_model: model comparison on a client-grouped split.
  • w06_validation_audit: the split before-and-after and the leakage audit.
  • w07_action_playbook: the ranked queue and review rules.
  • capstone: the notebook that mirrors this paper.

The warehouse is gated and its terms forbid redistributing it, so no data is in the repository. To rerun the work, request access to the dataset on Hugging Face, store a read token as a Colab secret named HF_TOKEN, and run each notebook top to bottom. Add an explicit ORDER BY to the feature query to make the split repeatable between runs.
